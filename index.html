Product case study · work.turing.com/jobs

Show every expert their match

Turing describes a network of millions of experts, yet it offers up to $750 to refer one US dermatologist. So the constraint is not headcount. It is getting the right expert to the right role, and that starts on the jobs page. Today a visitor cannot tell which of 307 roles they qualify for. I propose showing every person their match on every role.

Both sides of the marketplace

The jobs page is the supply side. The data it captures also serves demand across Turing's businesses, which is where the platform value sits. From the titles, most of the 307 roles look like AGI Advancement work. I could not tell from outside which roles serve enterprise projects, and I have not seen the demand-side tools, so this part is a proposal.

Three demand-side additions follow from this: a structured role intake (the posting template in bet 2), a supply view for project and sales leads, and an unmet-demand report built from empty searches and slow-to-fill roles. I have not scored the last two with RICE because they serve internal teams, and visitor reach does not apply.

The proposal: a match count on every role

People apply to everything because the page gives them no way to judge fit, and reviewers sort it out by hand. A visible match count steers people at the moment they choose. It needs no upkeep from them, unlike a cap on applications.

How it gets built
  1. Structure the roles. A language model extracts rate, hours, duration and must-haves from the 307 existing descriptions, and the project team confirms in one click. New roles use a template.
  2. Know the visitor. Logged in, use the profile. Logged out, ask three questions: skills, country, hours. That also captures a lead before the login wall.
  3. Show a count, not a percentage. "4 of 5" with the gap named is explainable, and the person can act on it. The same score orders the reviewer queue.

This counts only saved review time. The larger gain, which I cannot size from outside, is good experts who now find the role they fit.

How I would test it

By role, switching it on for half of a matched set of roles. Splitting visitors on the same role only moves applications around. The check on the score is acceptance rate by match band: if "5 of 5" is not accepted far more often than "2 of 5", the score is wrong.

Who comes to this page

I grouped visitors by what is going on in their head when they land. A doctor and a developer with the same doubt behave the same way on the page. These four groups come from my walkthrough and the kinds of roles listed, so I would check them against funnel data and a few applicant calls.

The skeptical expert

Senior doctor, lawyer or PhD with a full-time job

"Is this genuine, and is it worth my evenings?"
Behavior
Arrives from a referral or a LinkedIn post. Opens one or two roles and looks for pay and hours. Will not create an account just to find out.
We lose them
On the role page when there is no rate, and at the login box.
Business value
The scarcest supply. These are the roles carrying $500 to $750 referral bonuses. Served by bet 2.

The rate shopper

Contract engineer who already uses other platforms

"What is the best rate for my skill right now?"
Behavior
Searches by skill. Wants to open five roles in tabs and compare them.
We lose them
Search gives nothing, or 100 loosely related roles. There is no pay to compare.
Business value
Large pool for coding work, with other platforms one tab away. Served by bets 1 and 2.

The hopeful applicant

Student, career switcher or side-income seeker, mostly on a phone

"Which of these can I get?"
Behavior
Scrolls the list, reads little, applies to many.
We lose them
We do not. They apply to roles they cannot do (Pixel 8 only, US only) and are rejected later.
Business value
High volume and a real cost today, because reviewers handle every ineligible application. Served by bet 3.

The returner

Has applied before or finished a project

"Anything new for me since last time?"
Behavior
Runs the same search on every visit and checks Newest.
We lose them
No posted dates, expired roles still listed, no alerts. They have to keep checking, and many stop.
Business value
Already vetted, so the cheapest expert to place again. Served by bet 4.

The evidence, and the wider option list

The match count is built from bets 2 and 3: role facts in structured form, and eligibility checked before the form. Bet 4 follows once profiles exist. Bet 1 comes last and only for scarce roles. For each bet: what I saw on the live site, why it matters to the business, and what I would build.

Bet 1

Reach experts for scarce roles

Sign-up nowRole pages later
What I saw
  • The server response for /jobs is a "404 Page Not Found" shell tagged noindex. Roles load afterwards in the browser.
  • A role is a query parameter (?jobId=115452) with the same title as the list, so a shared link previews as a generic page.
  • No JobPosting markup and no sitemap.
  • An empty search offers only "Clear filters". A visitor with no match has no way to leave their details.
Why it matters
  • People search for work like "remote dermatology AI reviewer". Turing cannot appear there, so niche supply depends on paid channels and referral bonuses.
  • Every visitor who leaves unnamed is supply Turing paid to attract and cannot contact when the right project opens.
What I would build
  • A 30-second "join the talent pool" sign-up on empty results and on the list: email and skills only.
  • One server-rendered page per role, with JobPosting markup and a sitemap, after confirming noindex is not deliberate.
  • Shared role links that preview the role's title and pay.

Bet 2

Fill roles faster

Now
What I saw
  • Cards show a $100 to $750 badge with no label. It is the referral bonus. The Android rater role pays $25/hour, stated mid-description.
  • "Image Editing QA" says only "Its a pay per task". "Domain Expert – Python" lists no pay.
  • ML engineer returns no roles. Results take 3 to 4 seconds and the counter reads "All 0" meanwhile.
  • Apply Now opens "Welcome back" for a first-time visitor, with nothing on steps, tests or payment. On mobile the Apply button scrolls away.
Why it matters
  • A project bills only when its experts start. Each day a role stays open delays that.
  • Pay and time commitment are the first questions for contract work. Senior experts leave first when rates are unclear.
  • The Apply click is the highest-intent moment on the site, and it meets a login box.
What I would build
  • One release, "decision-ready listings": rate, hours per week, duration, start date and eligibility on every card and role, made mandatory at posting.
  • Referral bonus labeled as "Refer and earn".
  • Search that handles short forms (ML, JS, QA), ranks title matches first and never shows "0" while loading.
  • A "how applying works" note before login, sign-up shown first to new visitors, and a sticky Apply button on mobile.

Bet 3

Cut cost per accepted expert

Now
What I saw
  • The Android rater role lists one required skill, "Communication". The real gate is a Pixel 8 or newer on Android 17, several paragraphs down.
  • Country limits live in titles: "Business Analyst - US", "Dermatology (USA)". There is no country filter.
  • "Domain Expert – Python" is tagged Python and asks for 10+ years of CAD and simulation work.
Why it matters
  • Anyone can believe they qualify, so they apply. Each ineligible application costs reviewer time before it is rejected.
  • Wrong titles and skill tags pull in the wrong people and are poor inputs for any matching model.
What I would build
  • A short eligibility check before the form for roles gated by device, language or country.
  • Filters and sort for country, pay, hours and duration, with country defaulted from the visitor's location.
  • Separate must-have and nice-to-have requirements in the posting template.

How the expected returns were calculated

Each priority in the PRD carries an expected return. This is the working behind each one. The inputs are my assumptions, since I do not have Turing's numbers. The two P0 items cost about 19 person-weeks together (11 + 8), a team of four for about five weeks.

1 · P0 · Role facts on every role

Not sized. Return = days saved per role × experts per role × billing per expert-day. I have none of these inputs from outside. It is likely the largest return of all. The 11 person-weeks is my build estimate.

Prevented if the match count halves theseabout 165 hours a month

The 8 person-weeks is my build estimate.

3 · P1 · Reviewer queue ordered by match

No separate number. The queue is how the 165 hours from the match count are actually realized, so I have not counted it twice.

4 · P1 · Filter and "how applying works" note

Not sized. Return = visitors who click Apply × share who leave at the login box × share recovered. It needs Turing's funnel data.

5 · P2 · Alerts when a new role matches

Not sized. Return = roles filled by returning experts × about $300 not spent finding a new one. It needs the repeat-visit rate.

How I would measure it

  • Main number: experts accepted onto projects per 1,000 visitors. It must not fall.
  • Check on the score: acceptance rate by match band.
  • Leading signal: eligible applications per role in its first 7 days.
  • Cost: reviewer hours and acquisition spend per accepted expert.
  • Guardrail: share of applications rejected for eligibility must fall.
  • Test design: by role, on half of a matched set of roles. If "5 of 5" is not accepted far more often than "2 of 5", the score is wrong.

Risks, and what I could not see

  • A wrong low score could put off a good candidate, which is why nobody is blocked from applying.
  • Clients may not allow pay to be shown. In that case the role shows a band.
  • Turing's numbers. The size of each bet is a hypothesis, and the link to revenue runs through supply and fill time. I have not measured it.
  • The flow after sign-up. I did not create an account.
  • Whether pay and indexing are restricted by client contracts.
  • What Googlebot receives. The site sits behind a bot filter, so Search Console should confirm the noindex finding.
